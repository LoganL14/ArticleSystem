## IV. NUMERICAL RESULTS

In the main text, we present results for H 2 O, N 2 and hydrogen chains which are three widely popular benchmark systems. Additional cases such as CO and BeH 2 are summarized in the Appendix. All systems studied here exhibit qualitatively similar behavior, consistently achieving chemical accuracy.

In Fig. 2, we present results for H 2 O using the 6-31G atomic basis with a standard frozen-core approximation that freezes the oxygen 1 s orbital, while in Fig. 3 we show results for N 2 with the STO-6G basis. In both cases, the bond length is stretched symmetrically while keeping the bond angle fixed. From these data, we identify two key advantages of the method.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Rapid progress in superconducting quantum hardware, including Google's square lattice architectures [1] and IBM's heavy hex architectures [2], is making it increasingly feasible to execute sophisticated algorithms for quantum many body problems [3]. Problems whose Hilbert space dimension grows exponentially with system size N , such as ground state preparation, are particularly attractive targets. Quantum devices offer two key advantages: they bypass the severe memory constraints that limit classical simulation, and they provide a path to accurate treatment in strongly correlated regimes where classical methods degrade. For example, coupled cluster with perturbative triples (CCSD(T)) is widely regarded as the gold standard near equilibrium [4] but loses reliability in a strongly correlated regime. More advanced approaches, such as auxiliary field quantum Monte Carlo (AFQMC) [5-8], can help, although they are affected by the fermionic sign problem and are sign free only in special symmetry cases [9, 10]. These considerations motivate efficient quantum algorithms tailored to near term devices for addressing classically hard cases.

In the domain of quantum algorithms, many variational approaches have been proposed prior to this work [11-19], offering alternatives to other philosophies such as imaginary-time evolution [20-22] and subspace methods [23, 24].
